Can a Sleep Experiment Really Tell You What Works?

By Daniel Sebastian Montgomery · October 2026

Most people judge a sleep change by comparing one rough week to a fuzzy memory of "before." A sleep experiment replaces that guess with a measured comparison: a baseline week, then matched blocks of nights where only one thing changes. It feels slow, but it's close to the only way to find out whether a new pillow, an earlier bedtime, or a noise machine actually moved anything — or whether your sleep was going to improve regardless.

What is a sleep experiment, exactly?

It's a self-test where you change one variable, hold it steady for a fixed block of nights, and compare that block against an equal stretch of nights without the change. Researchers call the small-scale version an n-of-1 trial, and it's not just a hobbyist idea — a referral service running double-blind, multiple-crossover n-of-1 trials completed 57 trials, 50 of which produced a definite answer, and 39% changed the physician's plan (Guyatt et al.). The same logic can scale down to a bedroom.

Why did your last sleep fix seem to work?

Partly because expecting an improvement can produce one on its own. A meta-analysis of 32 drug trials for insomnia, covering nearly 4,000 participants, found that placebo groups reproduced 63.56% of the drug response — visible in sleep lab recordings, not just in how people described their nights (Winkler & Rief). Add to that regression to the mean: an unusually bad week tends to be followed by a more average one regardless of what you do, and the effect is stronger the worse your starting point was (Barnett et al.). Together, those two forces can make almost any change look like it helped for a week or two.

How many nights do you actually need?

Seven nights per condition is a reasonable default, though the data suggests you could get away with fewer. In a dataset of over 107,000 nights from more than 1,000 adults, five nights were enough to reach "very good" reliability for a weekly average of total sleep time (Lau et al.). Seven just leaves room for a full weekday-weekend mix plus one night to spare if something disrupts your sleep. That same data also showed that judging night-to-night variability, rather than averages, may need 11 to 18 nights — more than a short home experiment can reasonably cover.

Tip

Run a baseline week before changing anything, and avoid starting it on your worst night — doing so stacks the deck toward an "improvement" that would have happened anyway.

Can you trust a wrist tracker for this?

Mostly, for the purpose of comparing two blocks against each other. A pooled analysis of 24 studies and nearly 800 participants found wrist and watch trackers underestimated total sleep time by about 16.9 minutes on average compared with lab measurement (Lee et al.). That kind of steady bias tends to apply to both of your test blocks equally, so it can largely cancel out when you subtract one from the other. What it can't fix is night-to-night scatter, which is why single nights are unreliable and full blocks matter more.

Should you check your sleep score every morning?

It's probably better not to during the test itself. In one study, people with insomnia who were given made-up sleep-efficiency scores showed lower alertness and more fatigue by evening when told the number was poor — even though the score wasn't real (Gavriloff et al.). Checking a score daily can turn your own expectations into part of what you're trying to measure.

Watch out

Reading a nightly sleep score mid-experiment may bias how you feel the next day — save the review for after each block ends.

What should a sleep experiment actually measure?

Whatever outcome the change could plausibly affect — not necessarily the outcome you care most about. A review of nine studies and 555 participants found pillow design didn't meaningfully change sleep quality, but it was linked to less neck pain and disability (Pang et al.). A fixed-time morning diary, similar to the standardized Consensus Sleep Diary (Carney et al.), filled in before checking any device, is a solid baseline tool regardless of what you're testing.

Which change is worth testing first?

Cheap and reversible changes first — fixed wake time, morning light, caffeine cutoff — since a sleep experiment costs real time no matter what you test. Bedding swaps, like trying a firmer option such as the FirmNest Extra Firm Pillow, are a reasonable next step since they're a single clean change, but let a few nights pass for new foam to settle before judging it.

If poor sleep or persistent neck or shoulder pain continues despite a fair test, it's worth discussing with a doctor rather than cycling through more at-home changes. Frequently asked questions

Does a sleep experiment need a baseline week?

It helps a lot. Because an unusually bad stretch tends to be followed by a more average one on its own, starting your test during your worst week can make nearly anything look effective. A baseline week gives you a real comparison point instead of relying on memory of how things "normally" are.

Is two weeks long enough to test something?

Often, yes. Data from over 100,000 tracked nights found five nights reached strong reliability for a weekly sleep average, so a seven-night block per condition, plus a baseline week, is a reasonable minimum for most single-variable tests.

Can I test two sleep changes at the same time?

Not if you want a clear answer. Changing two things at once gives you one result with two possible explanations. It's slower but more useful to test the cheaper or easier change first, then move to the next one.

Are wrist trackers reliable enough to use?

For comparing two blocks, generally yes, even though they can run several minutes off true sleep time. That steady bias tends to affect both halves of the comparison equally, so the difference between them often still holds up — just avoid judging anything from a single night.

What if a sleep experiment shows no difference?

That's still a useful outcome. It means the change probably isn't worth its cost for you, and you can move on to testing the next candidate instead of continuing to credit something that likely isn't doing much.
