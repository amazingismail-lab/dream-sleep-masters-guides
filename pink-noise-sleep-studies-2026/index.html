Pink Noise for Sleep: What the 2026 Studies Actually Show

By Daniel Sebastian Montgomery · September 2026

Two studies published in 2026 both tested pink noise for sleep, and at first glance they seem to contradict each other. One found that short, precisely timed bursts of pink noise appeared to strengthen a brain process tied to deep sleep. The other found that playing pink noise all night reduced REM sleep in healthy adults. The difference isn't whether pink noise "works" — it's how it was used.

Does pink noise actually help you sleep?

It depends entirely on how it's delivered. A 2026 MIT study and a 2026 University of Pennsylvania trial both used pink noise, but one tested brief, timed bursts and the other tested a sound machine running all night — and they reached different conclusions.

What did the MIT study find?

MIT News reported on 9 September 2026 that short bursts of pink noise, timed to a specific phase of slow-wave (deep) sleep, increased the amplitude of those slow brain waves in healthy volunteers, and cerebrospinal fluid movement grew along with them. The work, from Laura Lewis's lab, builds on earlier findings that fluid waves in the brain are coupled to deep-sleep brain rhythms. Lewis said the team hopes to test the technique in people with insomnia or conditions marked by protein build-up. Importantly, the stimulus was a brief, precisely triggered sound in a monitored lab setting — not a continuous hiss — and the study measured a brain signal, not whether people felt more rested.

What did the Penn study find?

A trial published in the journal Sleep, led by Mathias Basner's team at Penn's Perelman School of Medicine, put 25 healthy adults through seven nights each of quiet, environmental noise (like aircraft sound), continuous pink noise, noise plus pink noise, and noise plus earplugs. Environmental noise cut deep sleep, as expected. Continuous pink noise reduced REM sleep, and pairing it with environmental noise made sleep structure worse overall despite small improvements in deep sleep and fragmentation. Subjective sleep quality, alertness, and mood were also worse after both noise and pink-noise nights.

Watch out

The Penn authors wrote that the REM-sleep effect should "caution against the widespread and indiscriminate use of broadband noise," according to Penn's own summary of the trial.

Why do the two studies seem to disagree?

They don't test the same thing, which is why the results aren't really contradictory. MIT's bursts nudged one brain rhythm for a moment during deep sleep; Penn's pink noise ran continuously through every stage, including the REM-heavy second half of the night. It's worth noting the Penn trial was small — 25 healthy young adults over seven lab nights — so the authors themselves call for more research on noise colour, volume, and long-term real-world use.

Can pink noise harm REM sleep?

In the Penn trial, continuous pink noise exposure did reduce REM sleep in healthy adults. Penn's summary points out that REM disruption is also seen in depression, anxiety, and Parkinson's disease, and that young children — who spend a larger share of the night in REM — may be more vulnerable, which is relevant given how common sound machines are in nurseries.

Tip

Earplugs "mitigated nearly all" of the negative effects of environmental noise in the Penn trial, except at the loudest noise level tested — worth considering if masking outside sound is your main goal.

Should you turn off your sound machine at night?

If you use pink or white noise mainly to block outside noise, the Penn data suggests earplugs may do that job without the REM cost. If you just like the sound, using a timer that switches it off after you fall asleep, rather than letting it run all night, may reduce exposure during REM-heavy hours. Is pink noise safe for babies and young children?

It may carry more risk for them than for adults, since children spend more of the night in REM sleep and REM disruption is the effect the Penn trial flagged. Neither 2026 study tested infants directly, so this is an inference from adult data, not a direct finding. If a child or adult has ongoing sleep problems, it's worth talking to a doctor rather than relying on a sound machine alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is pink noise the same as white noise?

No. White noise has equal energy across all frequencies, while pink noise has more energy at lower frequencies, which many people describe as deeper or softer. Both 2026 studies discussed here used pink noise specifically, so their findings shouldn't be assumed to apply identically to white noise.

Did the MIT study prove pink noise improves sleep quality?

Not directly. It showed that timed bursts increased slow-wave brain activity and fluid movement in healthy adults, which the researchers consider potentially restorative. It didn't measure whether participants felt more rested, and the stimulus was a lab-controlled burst, not the constant sound from a typical sound machine.

Does this mean I should stop using a sound machine?

Not necessarily. The Penn trial tested continuous, all-night exposure over just seven nights, and the authors call for more research rather than an outright ban. If it helps you fall asleep, using it at lower volume or on a sleep timer may be a reasonable middle ground.

Why did earplugs work better than pink noise in the Penn trial?

Earplugs reduced incoming sound rather than adding more of it, so they blocked disruptive environmental noise without introducing continuous broadband sound of their own. In the trial, earplugs "mitigated nearly all" of the negative effects except at the highest noise level tested.

Who should be most cautious about all-night pink noise?

Young children may warrant more caution, since they spend more time in REM sleep, the stage the Penn trial found was reduced. Anyone with persistent insomnia, mood changes, or unrefreshing sleep should raise it with a doctor rather than adjusting sound settings alone.
