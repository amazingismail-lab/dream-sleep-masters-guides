Do Fall-Asleep Tricks Work? A Look at the Evidence

By Daniel Sebastian Montgomery · October 2026

Short answer: partly. The ingredients behind popular fall-asleep tricks — muscle release, slow breathing, mental distraction — have some real research behind them. The packaged routines themselves, with their fixed counts and two-minute promises, mostly haven't been tested as a whole. What's been studied is the mechanism, not the branding.

What are the most popular fall-asleep tricks?

The military method, 4-7-8 breathing, and cognitive shuffling are the three that dominate searches, and they don't all do the same thing. The military method is a body-scan sequence — release the face, drop the shoulders, let the chest go, release the legs, hold a blank image. 4-7-8 breathing is a fixed count: inhale four, hold seven, exhale eight. Cognitive shuffling asks you to picture a stream of random, unconnected objects. One works on muscles, one on breath, one on attention — which is why the evidence for each looks so different.

Does the military method actually work?

Not as a branded two-minute routine — there's no clinical trial of that specific sequence. It traces back to Relax and Win, a 1981 coaching book by track coach Bud Winter (Open Library), and the two-minute figure appears to have travelled with the technique rather than coming from a study. What has been tested is its underlying mechanism, progressive muscle relaxation. A 2026 meta-analysis pooling 14 randomised trials and 957 adults found a large improvement in subjective sleep quality (Frontiers in Public Health), though the trials disagreed with each other considerably, and the benefit wasn't statistically significant for adults 55 and older.

Tip

Muscle relaxation is the best-supported ingredient in any of these tricks, but it was tested as a repeated practice over sessions, not a one-off fix for a single rough night.

Does 4-7-8 breathing shorten how long it takes to fall asleep?

The evidence genuinely conflicts here. One study of 14 self-reported insomniacs found that 20 minutes of slow paced breathing reduced sleep onset time, awakenings, and time spent awake overnight (Psychophysiology). But a crossover trial of 20 young adults, measured across 40 sleep-lab nights, found slow breathing raised heart rate variability immediately yet had no measurable effect on sleep latency at all (Scientific Reports). Both studies are small, and a 2026 systematic review of bedtime slow-breathing techniques suggests the question is still open (Sleep Medicine Reviews). Breathing exercises may calm your nervous system reliably without necessarily getting you to sleep faster.

Is there solid evidence for cognitive shuffling?

Its direct evidence is thin. The main study behind it is a 2015 conference presentation explicitly labeled "preliminary results" (SFU Summit), and its creator later published a 2019 abstract proposing the technique be tested properly against alternatives rather than reporting that it already had been (Sleep Medicine). The idea borrows credibility from older research: a 2002 trial of 41 people with insomnia found that using imagery to distract from unwanted pre-sleep thoughts shortened reported sleep onset compared with a no-distraction control (Behaviour Research and Therapy00012-2)). That's a one-night study, so it points in a direction more than it settles anything.

Note

None of these techniques were studied as instant fixes. They were tested as practices repeated over multiple nights or weeks, so judging one by a single bad night isn't a fair test.

Do sleep guidelines actually recommend any of these?

Only indirectly. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 guideline gives relaxation therapy a conditional recommendation as a stand-alone treatment, but it doesn't endorse any specific branded sequence (AASM guideline). The guideline is notably blunt about generic sleep-hygiene advice, suggesting clinicians not rely on it alone, as the AASM's announcement explains plainly: "sleep hygiene recommendation do not constitute an effective stand-alone therapy" (AASM).

What else decides whether a trick works for you?

These techniques only target mental arousal — racing thoughts, replayed conversations — which is one possible cause of a slow night, not the only one. If a room is too warm, light is leaking in, or a pillow leaves your neck at an awkward angle (a contour shape like the Aeris Contour Pillow is one way people try to fix that last part), no amount of counting breaths will solve it. Frequently asked questions

Which fall-asleep trick has the most evidence?

Progressive muscle relaxation, the mechanism behind the military method, has the strongest backing. It's the only one tied to a conditional AASM recommendation and the only one with a large pooled meta-analysis — 14 trials and 957 adults showing improved sleep quality, though the effect wasn't significant for people over 55.

Is the military method's two-minute claim backed by research?

No clinical trial has tested the branded two-minute sequence itself. The figure comes from a 1981 coaching book, not a study. The muscle-release practice underneath it has real evidence; the specific timing claim does not.

Can 4-7-8 breathing help even if I don't have insomnia?

It may help you feel calmer regardless. One study found it reduced heart rate variability measures in healthy young adults without changing how fast they fell asleep, so it can be a low-risk relaxation habit even without a guaranteed speed effect.

How many nights should I try a technique before deciding it doesn't work?

More than one. The trials behind these tricks delivered the practice over multiple sessions or weeks, so a single restless night isn't a reliable test of whether a technique works for you.

When should trouble falling asleep be checked by a doctor?

If difficulty sleeping persists for weeks, happens most nights, or starts affecting your daytime functioning, it's reasonable to talk to a doctor rather than relying on self-help techniques alone.
