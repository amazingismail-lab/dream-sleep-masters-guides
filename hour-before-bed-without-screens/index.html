No Screens an Hour Before Bed: What the Research Shows

By Daniel Sebastian Montgomery · October 2026

"No screens an hour before bed" is the sleep advice almost everyone has heard and almost no one explains. The research behind it points less at the glow of a phone and more at what that glow tends to replace: a fixed, on-time bedtime.

Does avoiding screens for an hour actually help you fall asleep?

It may help indirectly, by protecting your bedtime rather than by blocking light. A 2025 study in JAMA Network Open followed 122,058 adults and found that daily screen use in the hour before bed went with bedtimes roughly 18.8 minutes later on workdays and about 7.6 fewer minutes of sleep, plus a 33% higher rate of self-rated poor sleep quality. The study is observational, so it can't fully separate "screens delayed bedtime" from "late sleepers use screens more" — but the size and consistency of the pattern are hard to wave away.

Is it the blue light, the content, or just lost time?

Mostly lost time, based on how the effects stack up. Across 11 experimental studies, bright screen light delayed falling asleep by only about 2.7 minutes on average. A separate set of 7 studies found that alerting content, like games, cost around 3.5 minutes more sleep than calmer content such as TV. Melatonin suppression appears to need roughly 1,000–2,000 lux, while a phone or laptop screen puts out only about 80–100 lux at the eye — far below daylight's 100,000 lux. The minutes that actually disappear tend to come from scrolling past the point you meant to stop, not from the screen's brightness.

Tip

If light and content cost minutes but open-ended scrolling costs tens of minutes, a night-mode filter is solving the smaller problem. Setting an alarm for when the wind-down hour starts addresses the bigger one.

Where did the "one hour" rule come from?

It comes from an expert panel's caution, not a clock-tested threshold. In 2023, the National Sleep Foundation convened 16 experts who reviewed thousands of articles down to 35 experimental studies and voted on 10 statements about screens and sleep, reaching consensus on only 5. Every statement that named an age group named children and adolescents — not adults. That doesn't make the rule pointless for grown-ups, but it does mean "one hour" functions more as a planning margin than a scientifically fixed number.

Does a Kindle, TV, or audiobook count as a screen?

It depends more on whether the activity has a natural stopping point than on whether it emits light. An e-reader with a dim front light sits close to a paper book in terms of light exposure. A TV episode with autoplay turned off ends on its own; a social feed, by design, doesn't. A video game can combine both lower-light risk and the alerting-content effect at once. Judging each activity by "does this end by itself" is a more useful filter than "is this technically a screen."

What should you do instead during that hour?

A few options have some evidence behind them, modest as it is. A warm bath or shower around 40–42.5°C, finished 1–2 hours before bed, was linked to falling asleep about 10 minutes faster in a review that screened 5,322 studies — timing before bedtime, not right at it, seems to matter. Dimming overhead lights may do more than filtering a phone screen, given the lux gap mentioned above. Staying out of bed until you actually intend to sleep is also part of stimulus control, a technique the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 guideline supports as a recommended treatment component for insomnia.

Note

Low-stakes reading with a chosen stopping point can work well for some people — not because paper is special, but because a chapter, unlike a feed, has an end.

How do you build a wind-down hour you'll actually keep?

Start from your required wake time, subtract the sleep you need to get bedtime, then subtract another hour for when the wind-down begins — and set the alarm on that start time, not on bedtime itself. By the time a bedtime alarm sounds, the delay has usually already happened. When is a bad wind-down hour not the real issue?

It's not the issue when the problem doesn't match a late bedtime — for example, falling asleep quickly but waking at 3 a.m., waking unrefreshed after eight full hours, loud snoring with breathing pauses, or months of lying awake on a stable schedule. Those patterns may point to something a schedule tweak won't fix, and they're worth discussing with a doctor rather than troubleshooting alone. On the pillow side, some readers find that neck or shoulder discomfort — not screens — is what's keeping them awake once the lights are off; products like the Aeris Contour Pillow address loft and positioning rather than the pre-bed hour itself.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a completely screen-free hour before bed?

Probably not a literal ban. The strongest evidence links screen use to a later bedtime, not to screens themselves being harmful in small doses. A fixed point in the evening after which nothing open-ended starts may matter more than banning devices outright.

Does a blue-light filter fix the problem?

It addresses the smallest piece of it. Screen light was linked to about 2.7 minutes of delay in falling asleep across 11 studies, while the bedtime-delay effect runs into tens of minutes. A filter can help a little, but it doesn't touch the bigger issue of lost time.

Is a Kindle different from a phone for sleep purposes?

Mostly in whether it has a stopping point. An e-reader's light output is low, but the real risk factor with any device is whether the activity can run indefinitely. A chapter ends; a feed doesn't.

Why does advice always say "one hour"?

No trial tested one hour specifically. It's drawn from expert panel guidance, and even that panel only reached consensus on statements about children and teens, not adults — so treat it as a reasonable buffer, not a precise rule.

When should I see a doctor instead of adjusting my evening routine?

If you notice loud snoring, breathing pauses, unrefreshing sleep despite enough hours, or ongoing insomnia despite a steady schedule, those symptoms may need evaluation a bedtime change can't provide, so it's worth checking with a doctor.
